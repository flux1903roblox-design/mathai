# Gamification and Motivation Mechanics in Learning Apps for Ages 11-15 (Math Focus): Evidence-Graded Summary

Evidence grade key used throughout (assigned by this researcher, not by the sources):
- **A** = several RCTs or meta-analyses that include school-age students, with consistent direction.
- **B** = meta-analytic or experimental evidence, but mostly from adults or university students, or a single large school RCT.
- **C** = industry A/B data, single small studies, quasi-experiments, or extrapolation from neighbouring research.
- **D** = expert opinion, advocacy, or no direct evidence found.

## Key Question 1: What does research say about each mechanic (streaks/daily goals, XP/points, levels/paths/mastery maps, badges, leaderboards/leagues, lives/hearts, virtual currency/shop, avatars/mascots/pedagogical agents, sounds/haptics/celebrations, push notifications, social features)?

### Takeaway
No single mechanic has strong, child-specific evidence of its own. The strongest signals are these: (a) pure individual competition is the weakest social structure, and competition combined with collaboration does better; (b) penalties for mistakes (hearts) hurt progress, especially for beginners; (c) streaks lift engagement but backfire when broken unless the user can repair or forgive them; (d) decorative "seductive" extras reduce learning; (e) simple pedagogical agents give small gains. Most per-mechanic evidence comes from adults, university students or industry A/B tests, so it must be extrapolated to ages 11-15 with care.

### Cited Findings

#### Streaks and daily goals (grade: C)
- Duolingo's A/B test (blog post, 19 Nov 2020) separated the streak from the daily XP goal, so that one lesson a day keeps the streak alive. Results: +3.3% Day-14 retention, +1% daily active learners, and within 20 days a 10.5% relative increase in the share of daily learners on a streak (+19% among new learners). Fewer learners reached their daily goals, but "more learners were building daily habits and spending more time learning in aggregate." — [Duolingo Blog: Improving the streak](https://blog.duolingo.com/improving-the-streak/)
- In the same post, Duolingo reported that "just over half of our daily learners have a streak at least 7 days long, compared to about a third of our daily learners a year ago." — [Duolingo Blog](https://blog.duolingo.com/improving-the-streak/)
- Duolingo describes the Streak Freeze, which pauses the streak for a day, as designed to give flexibility, and says it "keeps learners dedicated to learning even when they need to skip a day or two of practice." No numbers are given. — [Duolingo Blog: habit-building research behind the streak](https://blog.duolingo.com/how-duolingo-streak-builds-habit/)
- Silverman & Barasch (2023, *Journal of Consumer Research* 49(6):1095), seven studies: intact streaks highlighted in behavioral logs increase subsequent engagement in the behavior. The paper frames its contribution as helping companies benefit from streaks "without incurring a cost (e.g., reduced engagement or abandonment) when those streaks are broken." — [OUP abstract](https://academic.oup.com/jcr/article-abstract/49/6/1095/6623414); [CU Boulder summary](https://www.colorado.edu/business/faculty-research/2023/04/19/or-track-how-broken-streaks-affect-consumer-decisions)
- A secondary summary of Silverman & Barasch says streaks "can backfire once a streak is broken, a limitation that can, however, be attenuated by the option to restore the lost streak." This is a citing paper's paraphrase and the samples are adult consumers. — [ResearchGate page/citing text](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/361658509_On_or_Off_Track_How_Broken_Streaks_Affect_Consumer_Decisions)

#### XP / points and levels (grade: B for performance quantity in adults; C/D for learning in children)
- Mekler et al. (2017, *Computers in Human Behavior*) tested points, levels and leaderboards one at a time in an image-annotation task. The game elements raised performance (output), but participants' "performance did not mirror their intrinsic motivation", and the elements did not significantly increase intrinsic motivation. The participants were adults on a non-learning task. — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0747563215301229); [UCLA SMHP summary](https://smhp.psych.ucla.edu/pdfdocs/gamil.pdf)
- A 2023 crowdsourcing study found that high leaderboard ranks motivated people "to only secure the outcome, i.e. their positions, rather than focus on the task itself". The authors add that "the motivations of the high-rankers and the low-rankers did not seem likely to be intrinsic ones." The participants were adults. — [Emerald, Internet Research 2023](https://www.emerald.com/intr/article/33/7/1/178330/How-leaderboard-positions-shape-our-motivation-the)

#### Levels / learning path / mastery-based practice (grade: B for adaptive practice with immediate feedback in grade 7; D for the visual "map" itself)
- ASSISTments, online 7th-grade math homework with immediate correctness feedback, hints and real-time teacher reports, was tested in a well-conducted RCT in Maine. It raised end-of-7th-grade state test scores (effect size 0.18). — [Arnold Ventures summary](https://www.arnoldventures.org/stories/a-replication-randomized-controlled-trial-rct-of-assistments-an-online-study-tool-to-improve-student-math-outcomes-in-seventh-grade); [SRI paper PDF](https://www.sri.com/wp-content/uploads/2021/12/2332858416673968.pdf)
- A pre-registered replication RCT in North Carolina found +0.10 SD (p=0.01) on the 8th-grade state math test one year after the program ended. That is roughly a 31% improvement over the expected annual gain. There was suggestive evidence (p<0.10) of a larger impact for higher-performing students. — [Arnold Ventures](https://www.arnoldventures.org/stories/a-replication-randomized-controlled-trial-rct-of-assistments-an-online-study-tool-to-improve-student-math-outcomes-in-seventh-grade); [WestEd](https://www.wested.org/support/efficacy-of-assistments-online-homework-support-for-middle-school-mathematics-learning/)
- Math Garden, an adaptive Dutch math app for children, used a 6-week experiment with preset success rates. The higher the preset success rate, "the more problems were attempted, and the larger the improvement in math performance." Math Garden can target success rates of 60% and above because it uses response time as extra ability information. — [Jansen et al. 2013, Learning and Individual Differences](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1041608012001951)

#### Badges / achievements (grade: C/D)
- Hanus & Fox (2015, *Computers & Education* 80:152-161) ran a semester-long university course that used badges, a leaderboard and coins. Students in the gamified course "showed less motivation, satisfaction, and empowerment over time than those in the non-gamified class", and lower intrinsic motivation was associated with lower final exam scores. Badges were not isolated from the other elements. — [ScienceDirect abstract](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0360131514002000); [Academia.edu full text summary](https://www.academia.edu/12454798/Assessing_the_Effects_of_Gamification_in_the_Classroom_A_Longitudinal_Study_on_Intrinsic_Motivation_Social_Comparison_Satisfaction_Effort_and_Academic_Performance)
- The Hanus & Fox authors suggest that "being forced to do something can decrease intrinsic motivation" and that gamification "may be more effective for individuals who have the option to engage with badges and leaderboards." — [Academia.edu](https://www.academia.edu/12454798/Assessing_the_Effects_of_Gamification_in_the_Classroom_A_Longitudinal_Study_on_Intrinsic_Motivation_Social_Comparison_Satisfaction_Effort_and_Academic_Performance)

#### Leaderboards and leagues (grade: B that pure competition is weakest; C for position effects on low performers)
- Clark, Tanner-Smith & Killingsworth (2016, *Review of Educational Research* 86(1):79-122) meta-analysed K-16 digital games. Games were associated with about +0.33 SD learning overall. Single-session play showed no benefit (g=0.08), while multiple sessions did (g=0.44). Single-player competitive games had the smallest effect (g=-0.06, n.s.). Single-player non-competitive games and collaborative team competition outperformed single-player competition. The authors conclude "competitive single-player structures may be least effective." — [ResearchGate full text](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/277630332_Digital_Games_Design_and_Learning_A_Systematic_Review_and_Meta-Analysis); [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.3102/0034654315582065)
- Sailer & Homner (2020): for behavioral outcomes, competitive-collaborative gamification outperformed no social interaction and outperformed mere competition (Q(1)=6.87, p<.01). Among high-rigor studies, competition plus collaboration showed a medium effect on motivation and "outperformed gamification environments that solely used competition." — [Springer, Educational Psychology Review](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-019-09498-w)
- Bai, Hew, Sailer & Jia (2021, *Computers & Education* 173:104297) studied 50 postgraduate online students. An absolute leaderboard intensified comparison and competitiveness more than a relative one. On the absolute board, performance and engagement were similar across positions, but higher position went with higher intrinsic motivation. On the relative board, the top third performed better. Bottom-third students "preferred anonymous (vs public) comparison on an absolute leaderboard but favoured public (vs anonymous) comparison on a relative leaderboard." — [ACM DL abstract](https://dl.acm.org/doi/abs/10.1016/j.compedu.2021.104297)
- In a 2025 experiment, starting leaderboard position (6th vs 25th) and trend were manipulated. Intrinsic motivation was highest for a high position with an upward trend. The paper's review also notes prior findings that "lower-ranked players did not show decreased motivation compared to a non-leaderboard condition," while other work found that learners in lower positions "might even tend to feel less competent." — [ScienceDirect, "The winner takes it all"](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1041608025002122)
- Bai et al. (2020) also documented learner anxiety and jealousy around competitive elements in their qualitative synthesis. This is reported by a citing paper, not checked against the original. — [ResearchGate citing text](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/265644737_Assessing_the_effects_of_gamification_in_the_classroom_A_longitudinal_study_on_intrinsic_motivation_social_comparison_satisfaction_effort_and_academic_performance)
- Christy & Fox (2014, *Computers & Education* 78:66-77) tested leaderboards in a virtual classroom as a source of stereotype threat and social comparison for women's math performance. Only the title and bibliographic data were retrieved. — [PsycNET](https://psycnet.apa.org/record/2014-34088-008)
- Duolingo's leagues are weekly global XP competitions with promotion and demotion. No published efficacy data was found. — [Duolingo Blog: leagues](https://blog.duolingo.com/duolingo-leagues-leaderboards/)

#### Lives / hearts / penalties for mistakes (grade: C for direct data, B for the underlying feedback principle)
- Duolingo (3 July 2025) explained why it replaced Hearts with Energy: "With our old system, Hearts, each mistake cost 1 heart for free learners. This was not the most effective way to support learning. It could be especially frustrating for beginners, who were still figuring things out and 2X more likely to run out of hearts mid-lesson." Duolingo says "our data shows [Energy] helps more learners complete lessons," especially "newer users and less engaged users who previously got stuck on mistakes." — [Duolingo Blog: Energy](https://blog.duolingo.com/duolingo-energy/)
- Energy is still monetized. Learners can "watch a rewarded ad to earn back some Energy, or buy an Energy refill using gems", and bonus energy is given for "adding a friend." Some users strongly objected to the change, but that evidence is anecdotal. — [Duolingo Blog](https://blog.duolingo.com/duolingo-energy/); [Android Authority](https://www.androidauthority.com/quitting-duolingo-energy-system-3599842/)
- Wisniewski, Zierer & Hattie (2020) meta-analysed 435 studies (k=994, N>61,000). "Reinforcement or punishment" feedback had the smallest effect (d=0.24), corrective feedback d=0.46, and high-information feedback d=0.99. — [Frontiers in Psychology / ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/338745455_The_Power_of_Feedback_Revisited_A_Meta-Analysis_of_Educational_Feedback_Research)

#### Virtual currency / shop (grade: D for any learning benefit; C for documented harms)
- An FTC complaint against Prodigy, a role-playing math game for grades 1-8, was filed on 19 Feb 2021 by 22 groups led by CCFC/Fairplay. It alleged that in 19 minutes of play there were 16 unique membership ads against 4 math problems, that Premium cost $59.88-$107.40 per year, and that Premium gave "coveted virtual swag" and faster levelling, with the status visible in school. These are advocacy allegations; no FTC outcome was found. — [Fairplay press release](https://fairplayforkids.org/feb-19-2021-advocates-to-ftc-prodigy-math-game-preys-on-kids-and-families/); [NBC News](https://www.nbcnews.com/tech/tech-news/child-protection-nonprofit-alleges-manipulative-upselling-math-game-prodigy-n1258294)
- Fairplay also alleged that non-members "literally walk in dirt while kids with memberships ride around on clouds," and that members "advance in the game faster, earning higher scores from completing the same tasks." — [Fairplay: 7 reasons](https://fairplayforkids.org/pf/prodigy/)

#### Avatars / mascots / pedagogical agents (grade: B for small positive learning effects of agents; D for avatar customization)
- Schroeder, Adesope & Gilbert (2013), 43 studies, N=3,088: overall g=0.19. Math g=0.27 (k=8), grades 4-7 g=0.56 (k=5, N=348) vs post-secondary g=0.12, and classroom g=0.68 (k=4). For low prior knowledge learners the effect was g=0.01 (n.s.). The authors warn of a possible novelty effect and of the small number of K-12 studies. — [PDF](http://debdavis.pbworks.com/w/file/fetch/96898947/schroeder%20adesope%20gilbert%20--%20how%20effective%20are%20pedagogical%20agents.pdf)
- Castro-Alonso et al. (2021) meta-analysed multimedia pedagogical agents (32 effect sizes, N=2,104): g+=0.20. 2D agents (g=0.38) tended to beat 3D agents (g=0.11), which fits the redundancy and coherence principles. — [JSTOR abstract](https://www.jstor.org/stable/48750366)
- Conflict: a 2026 PMC meta-analysis cites Schroeder et al. as d=0.66 and Castro-Alonso et al. as g=0.44. Both differ from the primary sources above (g=0.19 and g+=0.20), so the primaries should be preferred. — [PMC 2026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13603702/)

#### Sounds, haptics, celebration animations (grade: C, by extrapolation from instructional-design research)
- Sundararajan & Adesope (2020, "Keep it coherent") meta-analysed seductive details, meaning interesting but irrelevant additions to learning materials. They lowered learning performance (g=-0.33). The effect did not differ by format (audio, image, text), timing, size, placement or frequency. — [Economy of Meaning summary](https://theeconomyofmeaning.com/2020/03/02/how-a-cartoon-in-a-textbook-can-hurt-learning-a-new-meta-analysis-of-the-seductive-details-effect/); [QIC-WD summary](https://www.qic-wd.org/umbrella/seductive-details-in-training); [PsycNET record](https://psycnet.apa.org/record/2020-14793-001)
- The authors note that the meta-analysis "does not summarize emotional engagements resulting from the inclusion of seductive details." — [Economy of Meaning](https://theeconomyofmeaning.com/2020/03/02/how-a-cartoon-in-a-textbook-can-hurt-learning-a-new-meta-analysis-of-the-seductive-details-effect/)

#### Push notifications / reminders (grade: C/D for benefit; descriptive evidence of overload)
- Common Sense Media's "Constant Companion" study (2023) tracked preadolescent and adolescent smartphone use. Participants received a median of 237 notifications a day; about 23% arrived during school hours and 5% at night. Phone use during school hours had a median of 43 minutes, and adolescents (13+) were more likely to check their phone over 100 times a day. — [Common Sense report PDF](https://www.commonsensemedia.org/sites/default/files/research/report/2023-cs-smartphone-research-report_final-for-web.pdf); [press release](https://www.commonsensemedia.org/press-releases/teens-are-bombarded-with-hundreds-of-notifications-a-day)
- The ICO Children's Code lists "reward loops, continuous scrolling, notifications and auto-play features" as "sticky" features that extend engagement. — [ICO Standard 5](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/childrens-information/childrens-code-guidance-and-resources/age-appropriate-design-a-code-of-practice-for-online-services/5-detrimental-use-of-data/)

#### Social features (grade: B for collaboration and team structures)
- Sailer & Homner (2020) and Clark et al. (2016) both favour collaborative or team-competitive structures over individual competition (see the leaderboards section). — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-019-09498-w); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/277630332_Digital_Games_Design_and_Learning_A_Systematic_Review_and_Meta-Analysis)
- In Wisniewski et al. (2020), student-to-student feedback had d=0.85 (k=16), compared with teacher-to-student d=0.47 (k=812). — [ResearchGate (Frontiers)](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/338745455_The_Power_of_Feedback_Revisited_A_Meta-Analysis_of_Educational_Feedback_Research)

### Inferences
- **Streaks:** keep them, but make them forgiving. One small unit should keep the streak, freezes should exist, and a broken streak should be repairable. Avoid loss-framed messaging. Duolingo's own data shows that a softer streak increases habit formation, and consumer research shows broken streaks are the main risk. Evidence comes from adults and general users, not 11-15-year-olds (grade C).
- **XP/points:** likely to increase volume of practice. They are not a learning mechanism on their own and do not reliably build intrinsic motivation (grade B for adults, C for kids). XP should reward learning-relevant behaviour, such as correct retrieval after a delay or fixing an error, not raw time or taps.
- **Mastery path:** the best-supported structure for grade 7-8 is adaptive practice with immediate feedback, hints and teacher reporting (ASSISTments, grade B). The "map" is packaging. Difficulty should keep success rates fairly high, because higher success raised practice volume and gains in Math Garden.
- **Badges:** use sparingly and tie them to mastery or effort milestones. Make them optional and non-competitive. No isolated child evidence supports badges as a driver (grade C/D).
- **Leaderboards:** the evidence argues against global, individual, absolute leaderboards for this age. If a leaderboard is used, prefer class or team-based boards, relative or nearby ranks, effort-based metrics, anonymity or opt-out, and short cycles that reset. The evidence that low performers are harmed is mixed (grade C), but no study shows that low performers benefit from public absolute ranking.
- **Hearts/lives:** drop them in practice mode. Duolingo itself moved away from per-mistake penalties, beginners were hit hardest, and punishment-type feedback carries the least information (grade B/C).
- **Currency/shop:** cosmetic-only and earned-only at most. Never sell or buy learning progress, and never tie items to paid tiers visible to classmates. Children's apps commonly contain manipulative designs (grade C for harms, D for benefit).
- **Mascot/agent:** a simple 2D guide that gives hints or explanations has small positive evidence (grade B). A mascot used to guilt-trip or pressure children matches the "parasocial relationship pressure" category in Radesky et al. (see Key Question 4).
- **Celebrations/sounds:** keep them short, relevant and skippable, and put them after the answer feedback rather than during problem solving. This extrapolates from the seductive-details effect (g=-0.33, grade C).
- **Notifications:** default to few, teacher- or student-scheduled reminders, with none during school hours or at night unless the class assigned them. Teens already receive a median of 237 notifications a day, and the ICO treats data-driven notifications as potentially unfair "sticky" features (grade C/D).

### Gaps
- No study was found that isolates streaks, badges or leagues in children aged 11-15. Duolingo's streak and league data are not broken down by age, and Duolingo Math has no published efficacy data.
- No direct evidence was found comparing class-based and global leaderboards, or opt-in and mandatory leaderboards, in middle school.
- The findings of Christy & Fox (2014) on leaderboards, gender and math were not retrieved (title only).
- No research was found on haptics in learning apps, or on avatar customization and learning outcomes for this age.
- The FTC outcome of the Prodigy complaint was not found.
- Duolingo's Streak Freeze experiment numbers (beyond the qualitative claim) and its notification-optimization research (e.g., a bandit-algorithm paper) were not retrieved.

## Key Question 2: Effects on math anxiety, intrinsic vs. extrinsic motivation (overjustification), growth mindset and error-friendly feedback, spaced repetition/retrieval, interleaving, feedback timing, and worked examples

### Takeaway
The learning-science "engine" has much stronger evidence than the gamification "skin". Retrieval practice and spacing are rated high utility. Interleaved and spaced math practice produced d=0.83 in a 7th-grade RCT. Information-rich feedback (d≈0.99) beats reward or punishment feedback (d≈0.24). Feedback timing per se matters little in computer-based learning. Growth-mindset interventions alone show near-zero achievement effects in the highest-quality studies. Gamification tends to raise extrinsic motivation at least as much as intrinsic motivation, and long-running reward-heavy designs can erode intrinsic motivation. Evidence that gamification reduces math anxiety is weak.

### Cited Findings

#### Math anxiety
- In Math Garden, children practised for six weeks under different preset success rates (60%+ was feasible). Higher preset success led to more problems attempted and larger math improvement. The study's hypothesis was that experiencing high success would lower math anxiety and raise perceived competence. — [Jansen et al. 2013](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1041608012001951); [CiteSeerX PDF](https://citeseerx.ist.psu.edu/viewdoc/download?doi=10.1.1.836.9589&rep=rep1&type=pdf)
- Boaler (youcubed) claims that "for about one third of students the onset of timed testing is the beginning of math anxiety", that stress under time pressure blocks working memory (citing Beilock 2011 and Ramirez et al. 2013), and that math anxiety has been recorded in children as young as 5. This is an advocacy and position piece, and the causal claim that timed tests are "a major cause" is stronger than the cited evidence. — [YouCubed: Fluency Without Fear](https://www.youcubed.org/evidence/fluency-without-fear/); [Stanford GSE news](https://ed.stanford.edu/news/boaler-timed-tests-and-development-math-anxiety)
- Several small studies claim gamified tools reduce math anxiety in middle school. One is a conference paper reporting "preliminary observations"; another is a Turkish journal study reporting "significantly reduced mathematics anxiety". Both are low-quality and small-sample. — [ECGBL 2025 paper](https://papers.academic-conferences.org/index.php/ecgbl/article/view/4084); [DergiPark](https://dergipark.org.tr/en/pub/ijesp/article/1978755)
- A higher-level synthesis (see Key Question 3) reports that a 2026 meta-analysis of gamification in mathematics (Yao, 2026) found a non-significant affective effect (g=0.23), against a cognitive effect of g=0.45. — [Second-order meta-analysis, JMER](https://journal.privietlab.org/index.php/JMER/article/view/2343)

#### Intrinsic vs. extrinsic motivation (overjustification)
- Kurnaz & Koçtürk (2025, *Psychology in the Schools* 62:4997-5009) meta-analysed K-12 experimental studies: 31 studies, k=41, pooled g=0.654 on motivation, with I²=88.92. The effect was larger for extrinsic motivation (g=0.713, 95% CI 0.152-1.275) than intrinsic (g=0.638, 0.239-1.036). It was largest in secondary/middle school (g=1.015), then high school (0.821), then primary (0.309). — [Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/pits.70056)
- Hanus & Fox (2015) found that in a university course with badges, leaderboard and coins, intrinsic motivation fell over time relative to control, and this mediated lower final exam scores. — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0360131514002000)
- Mekler et al. (2017) found that points, levels and leaderboards raised output without significantly raising intrinsic motivation (adults). — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0747563215301229)
- Sailer & Homner (2020): the motivational effect of gamification (g=.36) was not robust in high-rigor studies (g=.22, n.s., k=7). Motivational effects were larger for interventions lasting up to half a year than for those lasting 1 day or less. — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-019-09498-w)
- Wisniewski et al. (2020) cite Hattie & Timperley's statement that rewards significantly undermine intrinsic motivation. In their meta-analysis, reinforcement/punishment feedback had d=0.24 and feedback effects on motivational outcomes were d=0.33. — [ResearchGate (Frontiers)](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/338745455_The_Power_of_Feedback_Revisited_A_Meta-Analysis_of_Educational_Feedback_Research)

#### Growth mindset and error-friendly feedback
- Macnamara & Burgoyne (2023, *Psychological Bulletin* 149(3-4):133-173) meta-analysed growth-mindset interventions. Across all studies the effect on achievement was d=0.05 (95% CI 0.02-0.09), and it was non-significant after correcting for publication bias. Studies that confirmed mindset change (13 studies, N=18,355) gave d=0.04 (n.s.), and the highest-quality studies (6, N=13,571) gave d=0.02 (n.s.). — [PsycNET](https://psycnet.apa.org/record/2023-14088-001); [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36326645/)
- This is contested. A separate 2023 meta-analysis, "A systematic review and meta-analysis of growth mindset interventions: For whom, how, and why might such interventions work?", argues for heterogeneous effects (title only retrieved). — [PubMed related](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36227318/)
- Duolingo's internal data shows per-mistake penalties stalled beginners, who were 2x more likely to run out of hearts. Moving to a system that rewards correct streaks helped more learners complete lessons. — [Duolingo Blog](https://blog.duolingo.com/duolingo-energy/)

#### Retrieval practice and spacing
- Dunlosky et al. (2013) rated practice testing and distributed practice as "high utility" because "they benefit learners of different ages and abilities", including in educational contexts. Interleaved practice, elaborative interrogation and self-explanation were rated "moderate utility". — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26173288/)
- The IES practice guide (Pashler et al., 2007; intended for grades 3-12) recommends spacing learning over time, with key content reviewed "after a delay of several weeks to several months", and using quizzing (pre-questions and quizzes to re-expose students to material). — [IES WWC Practice Guide](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/practiceguide/1); [Full PDF](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/Docs/PracticeGuide/20072004.pdf)

#### Interleaving
- Rohrer, Dedrick, Hartwig & Cheung (2020, *Journal of Educational Psychology*) ran a cluster RCT in 54 seventh-grade math classes. On an unannounced test one month later, interleaved-and-spaced worksheets outscored blocked practice, 61% vs 38% (d=0.83). — [PsycNET](https://psycnet.apa.org/doiLanding?doi=10.1037%2Fedu0000367); [IES award page](https://ies.ed.gov/use-work/awards/efficacy-study-interleaved-mathematics-practice)
- Dunlosky et al. (2013) report a classroom-style study on volumes of geometric solids in which accuracy one day later was "substantially higher" after interleaved practice (77%) than after blocked practice. Interleaving makes performance during practice worse while improving delayed performance. — [Dunlosky et al. PDF](https://www.whz.de/fileadmin/lehre/hochschuldidaktik/docs/dunloskiimprovingstudentlearning.pdf)
- A UC Merced document mentions an effect "notably smaller than Rohrer et al. (.18 vs. .83)" in 54 seventh-grade classrooms. It is unclear which study this refers to, which flags possible effect shrinkage at scale. — [eScholarship PDF](https://escholarship.org/content/qt9429f5h9/qt9429f5h9.pdf)

#### Feedback timing and type
- A meta-analysis of studies directly comparing immediate and delayed feedback in computer-assisted learning (HAL preprint) found "no reliable difference" for delays from seconds or a few items up to several days. Studies favouring delayed feedback tended to be older (pre-2012), to use text memorization and primary/secondary pupils, and to delay by more than 21 items. Studies favouring immediate feedback defined delay as a small number of items. Many studies confound timing with the retention interval. — [HAL preprint](https://hal.science/hal-05546645v1/file/Meta_HAL_submission.pdf)
- Wisniewski et al. (2020): overall d=0.48 (435 studies). High-information feedback (d=0.99) is far more effective than simple reinforcement or punishment (d=0.24). — [ResearchGate (Frontiers)](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/338745455_The_Power_of_Feedback_Revisited_A_Meta-Analysis_of_Educational_Feedback_Research)
- ASSISTments' immediate correctness feedback and hints produced a 0.18 SD gain in 7th grade (Maine RCT). — [Arnold Ventures](https://www.arnoldventures.org/stories/a-replication-randomized-controlled-trial-rct-of-assistments-an-online-study-tool-to-improve-student-math-outcomes-in-seventh-grade)

#### Worked examples
- IES Recommendation 2: "Interleave worked example solutions with problem-solving exercises." In practice, "have students alternate between reading already worked solutions and trying to solve problems on their own." The guide also recommends combining graphics with verbal descriptions, connecting abstract and concrete representations, and asking deep explanatory questions. — [IES WWC](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/practiceguide/1)

### Inferences
- For a grade 7-8 app, the biggest learning lever is the practice scheduler, not the reward layer: spaced review of earlier topics, interleaved mixed sets after initial blocked introduction, and retrieval-style questions. This is grade A/B evidence, including a 7th-grade RCT.
- Feedback should be immediate and information-rich: what was wrong, a hint, and a worked step. Gains come from informational content, not from rewards or penalties. Timing (immediate vs. end-of-set) can be chosen for UX reasons, though a short delay for review sets may add a spacing benefit.
- "Error-friendly" design should be built into mechanics: no penalty for mistakes, a free "review your mistakes" step, and retry. Growth-mindset slogans alone should not be expected to raise achievement (d≈0.02-0.05 in the best studies). The ethical and practical case for not penalizing errors rests on the feedback and Duolingo evidence, not on mindset research.
- On math anxiety: avoid default timers and speed pressure, and calibrate difficulty so most items are solvable, since higher success led to more practice and gains. Timed "fluency" modes, if any, should be optional. Claims that gamification reduces math anxiety rest on weak studies.
- Middle schoolers may be the group whose motivation responds most strongly to gamification (g≈1.0 in Kurnaz & Koçtürk). The effect is mostly extrinsic, heterogeneity is high, and long-term intrinsic effects are not established. Design should shift students from extrinsic rewards toward competence feedback over time, for example by making progress and mastery visible and fading reward intensity.

### Gaps
- The math-anxiety outcome of Jansen et al. (2013) was not confirmed in the retrieved text (only the practice and performance results were). The sample's grade levels were not retrieved.
- The classic Deci, Koestner & Ryan (1999) rewards meta-analysis was not retrieved in this session.
- Yeager et al.'s (2019) national mindset study was not retrieved, so the "for whom" counter-argument is represented only by a title.
- No study was found that directly tests a timer vs. no-timer manipulation in a gamified middle-school math app.
- The evidence-strength ratings IES assigned to each recommendation were not retrieved.

## Key Question 3: What do meta-analyses and reviews (Sailer & Homner 2020; Bai, Hew & Huang 2020; Zainuddin et al.; Hamari et al.), studies of Duolingo-like mechanics, and child-specific studies show overall?

### Takeaway
Meta-analyses consistently find small-to-medium positive average effects of gamification: about g≈0.5 on learning and g≈0.25-0.65 on motivation and behaviour. Heterogeneity is very high, and effects shrink in rigorous studies and after small-study adjustment, where the cognitive effect falls to about 0.3-0.5. The motivational and behavioural effects are the least robust. Duolingo-like evidence is mostly company A/B data on engagement, not learning.

### Cited Findings
- **Sailer & Homner (2020)**, *Educational Psychology Review* 32:77-112. Gamification effects were cognitive g=.49 (95% CI .30-.69, k=19, N=1,686), motivational g=.36 (k=16, N=2,246) and behavioural g=.25 (k=9, N=951). In high-rigor studies the cognitive effect held (g=.42, k=9, N=686), while motivational (g=.22, n.s.) and behavioural (g=.27, n.s.) effects did not. Game fiction moderated behavioural outcomes, and competition plus collaboration beat competition alone. Cognitive effects were significantly larger in school settings than in higher education. — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-019-09498-w); [APA PsycNET](https://psycnet.apa.org/record/2019-48906-001)
- **Bai, Hew & Huang (2020)**, *Educational Research Review* 30:100322. Across 30 interventions (3,202 participants, 24 studies), the overall effect on academic performance was g=0.504 (95% CI 0.284-0.723), with "no publication bias detected". The paper notes the controversy around gamification ("exploitationware"). — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1747938X19302908); [EdUHK repository](https://repository.eduhk.hk/en/publications/does-gamification-improve-student-learning-outcome-evidence-from-/)
- **Kurnaz & Koçtürk (2025)**, K-12 motivation: g=0.654 (k=41, 31 studies, I²≈89%). The effect was largest in secondary school (g=1.015) and smallest in primary (0.309). — [Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/pits.70056)
- **Second-order meta-analysis** of 55 meta-analyses published through October 2026 (JMER). Unadjusted averages were cognitive g=0.68, motivational/affective 0.58 and behavioural 0.56. Precision-effect adjustments reduced the cognitive estimate to 0.29-0.48, and the authors conclude the literature "probably overstates its cognitive benefits." It also cites Yao (2026) on mathematics: g=0.41 overall, 0.45 cognitive, 0.23 affective (n.s.), "with lower quality associated with larger effects." This journal is lesser-known, and Yao (2026) was not verified directly. — [JMER](https://journal.privietlab.org/index.php/JMER/article/view/2343)
- **Clark, Tanner-Smith & Killingsworth (2016)**, K-16 digital games: about +0.33 SD overall. Multi-session play had g=0.44 and single-session g=0.08. Individual competition was weakest. — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/277630332_Digital_Games_Design_and_Learning_A_Systematic_Review_and_Meta-Analysis)
- **Zainuddin et al. (2020a)**, *Educational Research Review* 30:100326, a systematic review, is cited as finding mostly positive results while noting that some research "lacks a theoretical explanation of the link between gamification and learning outcomes." — [Scholars' Mine thesis citing it](https://scholarsmine.mst.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=9073&context=masters_theses)
- A master's thesis meta-analysis (Missouri S&T) found overall g=0.43. Elementary learners showed a numerically larger effect, although education level was not a significant moderator. It also lists individual math studies with null learning effects: Watson-Huggins & Trotman 2019 reported no improvement in math scores, and Jagušt et al. 2018 reported no change in the number of correct attempts. — [Scholars' Mine](https://scholarsmine.mst.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=9073&context=masters_theses)
- Cautionary example: one 2023 meta-analysis reports a math-subject moderator of g≈2.0 based on only k=3, an implausibly large and unstable estimate. — [Ma & Shi 2023, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10591086/)
- **Duolingo-like mechanics**: the retrieved evidence is company-reported A/B results. Streak/daily-goal separation gave +3.3% D14 retention. Energy replacing Hearts gave more lesson completion, with no numbers published. These measure engagement and retention, not learning. — [Duolingo Blog: streak](https://blog.duolingo.com/improving-the-streak/); [Duolingo Blog: Energy](https://blog.duolingo.com/duolingo-energy/)

### Inferences
- A realistic planning assumption is that a well-designed gamification layer adds a small effect (roughly 0.2-0.4 SD) on top of good practice design. Its motivational benefit may be real in middle school but is fragile, and novelty effects are likely. Single-session or short pilots will overstate or misread effects, since multi-session effects differ.
- Because high-rigor studies show smaller motivational effects, the team should plan its own A/B or class-randomized evaluations. These should measure learning (delayed tests) and not only engagement metrics such as DAU or streak length, which Duolingo optimizes.
- The fact that cognitive effects are larger in school settings is encouraging for class use. Combined with ASSISTments' teacher-report model, it suggests that integration with teacher workflows matters.

### Gaps
- Hamari, Koivisto & Sarsa (2014, HICSS, "Does gamification work?") was not retrieved in full in this session. It is known as a 24-study review with mostly positive but context- and user-dependent results, but this was not verified here.
- Huang et al. (2020, *ETR&D* 68:1875-1901), which breaks effects down by game element, was referenced but its element-level numbers were not retrieved.
- Fadhli et al. (2020), a meta-analysis on gamification for children, was mentioned in the thesis but not retrieved.
- No independent peer-reviewed study of Duolingo's streak, league or energy mechanics with learning outcomes, or with child samples, was found.

## Key Question 4: What are the ethics and regulatory constraints for minors (dark patterns, streak pressure, loss aversion, notifications, leaderboard privacy, UK Children's Code, California AADC, COPPA, Israeli student-privacy rules)?

### Takeaway
Regulators treat engagement-maximizing "sticky" features (reward loops, notifications, loss-framed continuation prompts) and data-hungry nudges as risks for children. The UK Children's Code says to present continuation options neutrally "without suggesting that children will lose out," and to let children pause without losing progress. That directly constrains streak and loss-aversion design. In Israel, the Privacy Protection Authority and the Ministry of Education require privacy-by-design, MoE-approved suppliers, easy data deletion, and closed environments for under-13s. Amendment 13 to the Privacy Protection Law has been in force since 14 Aug 2025.

### Cited Findings

#### UK Age Appropriate Design Code (ICO Children's Code)
- **Standard 5 (Detrimental use of data):** "Strategies used to extend user engagement, sometimes referred to as 'sticky' features can include mechanisms such as reward loops, continuous scrolling, notifications and auto-play features." Designing "data-driven features which make it difficult for children to disengage... is likely to breach the Article 5(1)(a) fairness principle," for example "features which use personal data to exploit human susceptibility to reward, anticipatory and pleasure seeking behaviours, or peer pressure." — [ICO Standard 5](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/childrens-information/childrens-code-guidance-and-resources/age-appropriate-design-a-code-of-practice-for-online-services/5-detrimental-use-of-data/)
- The ICO directs services to:
  - "present options to continue playing or otherwise engaging with your service neutrally without suggesting that children will lose out if they don't";
  - "avoid using personal data in a way that incentivises children to stay engaged, such as offering children personalised in-game advantages... in return for extended play";
  - "introduce mechanisms such as pause buttons which allow children to take a break at any time without losing their progress."

  The ICO also says rewards and notifications are not banned outright: they can be designed "in a way that makes it easy for them to disengage without feeling pressurised or disadvantaged if they do so." — [ICO Standard 5](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/childrens-information/childrens-code-guidance-and-resources/age-appropriate-design-a-code-of-practice-for-online-services/5-detrimental-use-of-data/)
- **Standard 13 (Nudge techniques):** "Do not use nudge techniques to lead or encourage children to provide unnecessary personal data or turn off privacy protections." — [ICO Standard 13](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/childrens-information/childrens-code-guidance-and-resources/age-appropriate-design-a-code-of-practice-for-online-services/13-nudge-techniques/)
- The Code treats children differently by age band (0-5, 6-9, 10-12, 13-15, 16-17). A grade 7-8 cohort spans the 10-12 and 13-15 bands. — [Fenwick summary](https://www.fenwick.com/insights/publications/the-ico-age-appropriate-design-code-of-practice-steps-to-take-in-2021-to-protect-childrens-privacy)

### Inferences
- Streak and loss-aversion design is the area of highest regulatory and ethical risk. Messages such as "Your streak will die!", guilt-tripping mascot notifications, or paid streak repair map closely onto ICO Standard 5 ("lose out" framing, exploiting reward susceptibility) and Radesky's "parasocial relationship pressure." Forgiving streaks (freezes, repair by doing practice rather than paying) and neutral prompts are both better evidence-based UX and more compliant.
- Leaderboards in class use involve processing and displaying minors' performance data to peers. Under privacy-by-default (ICO, CAADCA defaults, Israeli PPA guidance), the default should be private, nickname-based or anonymous, with teacher control and student opt-out. Global leaderboards with strangers would be hard to justify for under-13s, given MoE's closed-environment rule.
- Monetization patterns copied from Duolingo (rewarded ads to refill energy, gems, bonus energy for "adding a friend") are high-risk for a children's school product. Ads and friend invites involve personal data and nudges (ICO Standard 13; COPPA opt-in for third-party ads). Paid cosmetics visible in class replicate the inequity alleged against Prodigy.
- For Israeli schools, the practical gating items are: getting onto the MoE-approved supplier list or Educational Catalog; meeting the MoE supplier security standard (easy deletion, secure storage, permission for sensors); a closed environment for under-13s; a PIA; and Amendment 13 compliance. Haptics and microphone or camera use would trigger the sensor-consent clause.
- Notifications to children should be minimal, non-loss-framed, quiet during school hours and at night, and easy to turn off. Defaults set by teachers or parents are safer than engagement-optimized algorithms.

## Key Question 5: What practical design recommendations follow from the evidence (forgiving streaks, mastery-based progress, class leaderboards with opt-out, no penalty for mistakes, etc.)?

### Takeaway
The evidence supports a "learning-science engine, light gamification skin" design: adaptive, spaced, interleaved practice with immediate information-rich feedback and teacher reporting, plus forgiving habit mechanics. Social features should be cooperative or team-based and private by default. Penalties, pay-to-win shops, loss-framed streak pressure and global individual leaderboards should be dropped.

### Cited Findings
(Each recommendation is followed by the evidence it rests on; all sources are cited above.)
- **Forgiving streaks.** A small minimum keeps the streak, and freezes or repair are available. Evidence: Duolingo's +3.3% D14 retention from decoupling streak and goal ([Duolingo](https://blog.duolingo.com/improving-the-streak/)); broken streaks reduce engagement, and restore options attenuate this ([Silverman & Barasch via ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/361658509_On_or_Off_Track_How_Broken_Streaks_Affect_Consumer_Decisions)); ICO "without suggesting that children will lose out" and pause without losing progress ([ICO Std 5](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/childrens-information/childrens-code-guidance-and-resources/age-appropriate-design-a-code-of-practice-for-online-services/5-detrimental-use-of-data/)).
- **No penalty for mistakes in practice.** Evidence: Duolingo dropped per-mistake Hearts because beginners were 2x more likely to run out and get stuck ([Duolingo](https://blog.duolingo.com/duolingo-energy/)); punishment/reinforcement feedback d=0.24 vs high-information d=0.99 ([Wisniewski et al.](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/338745455_The_Power_of_Feedback_Revisited_A_Meta-Analysis_of_Educational_Feedback_Research)).
- **Mastery-based, adaptive progress with high success rates and teacher reports.** Evidence: ASSISTments 7th-grade RCTs (+0.18; +0.10 a year later) ([Arnold Ventures](https://www.arnoldventures.org/stories/a-replication-randomized-controlled-trial-rct-of-assistments-an-online-study-tool-to-improve-student-math-outcomes-in-seventh-grade)); higher success rates led to more practice and gains in Math Garden ([Jansen et al.](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1041608012001951)).
- **Spaced and interleaved review built into the path.** Evidence: d=0.83 in a 7th-grade RCT ([Rohrer et al.](https://psycnet.apa.org/doiLanding?doi=10.1037%2Fedu0000367)); high utility of testing and spacing ([Dunlosky et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26173288/)); IES review after weeks to months ([IES](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/practiceguide/1)).
- **Worked examples alternating with practice problems.** Evidence: IES Recommendation 2 ([IES](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/practiceguide/1)).
- **Team or class cooperative goals rather than individual global leagues.** Evidence: competitive-collaborative > competitive ([Sailer & Homner](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-019-09498-w)); single-player competition weakest ([Clark et al.](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/277630332_Digital_Games_Design_and_Learning_A_Systematic_Review_and_Meta-Analysis)).
- **If a leaderboard is used: class-scoped, relative or near-peer, anonymous or opt-out.** Evidence: bottom-third students preferred anonymity on absolute boards ([Bai et al. 2021](https://dl.acm.org/doi/abs/10.1016/j.compedu.2021.104297)); low position can reduce perceived competence ([ScienceDirect 2025](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1041608025002122)); optional engagement may protect motivation ([Hanus & Fox](https://www.academia.edu/12454798/Assessing_the_Effects_of_Gamification_in_the_Classroom_A_Longitudinal_Study_on_Intrinsic_Motivation_Social_Comparison_Satisfaction_Effort_and_Academic_Performance)).
- **Simple 2D mascot as a helpful guide, not a pressure device.** Evidence: agent g≈0.19-0.20, 2D > 3D ([Schroeder et al.](http://debdavis.pbworks.com/w/file/fetch/96898947/schroeder%20adesope%20gilbert%20--%20how%20effective%20are%20pedagogical%20agents.pdf); [Castro-Alonso et al.](https://www.jstor.org/stable/48750366)); "parasocial relationship pressure" as a manipulative pattern ([Radesky et al.](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamanetworkopen/fullarticle/2793493)).
- **Short, skippable celebrations after feedback, not during problem solving.** Evidence: seductive details g=-0.33 ([summary](https://theeconomyofmeaning.com/2020/03/02/how-a-cartoon-in-a-textbook-can-hurt-learning-a-new-meta-analysis-of-the-seductive-details-effect/)).
- **Cosmetic, earned-only currency, or none; no pay-for-progress.** Evidence: Prodigy allegations ([Fairplay](https://fairplayforkids.org/feb-19-2021-advocates-to-ftc-prodigy-math-game-preys-on-kids-and-families/)); ICO on personalised in-game advantages ([ICO Std 5](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/childrens-information/childrens-code-guidance-and-resources/age-appropriate-design-a-code-of-practice-for-online-services/5-detrimental-use-of-data/)).
- **Minimal, neutral notifications with quiet hours.** Evidence: 237 notifications a day, 23% in school hours ([Common Sense](https://www.commonsensemedia.org/press-releases/teens-are-bombarded-with-hundreds-of-notifications-a-day)); ICO sticky-features guidance.
- **Israeli compliance by design.** MoE-approved supplier status, a closed environment for under-13s, easy data deletion, a PIA, and Amendment 13 compliance ([PPA guide](https://www.gov.il/BlobFolder/reports/students_privacy_guide/he/students_privacy_guide.pdf)).

### Inferences
Suggested tuning parameters. These are inferences, not tested values.
- **Streak:**
  - Counts any day with at least one short session (e.g., 3-5 problems).
  - Auto-freezes on weekends and school holidays (Israeli calendar), with 1-2 earnable freezes.
  - A broken streak can be "repaired" by completing a review set within 48 hours, never by payment.
  - No countdown or "you'll lose it" messages; show "longest streak" and "days practised this month" instead of only the current streak.
- **XP:**
  - Weight XP toward first-try-after-delay correct answers (spaced retrieval), error correction, and completing mixed or interleaved sets.
  - Cap daily XP from easy repeats to prevent grinding.
  - Show mastery per skill as the main progress signal.
- **Difficulty:** target high success rates. Math Garden supports targets of 60% and above, and higher success produced more practice; a range of about 70-85% is a reasonable starting assumption to A/B test. Use optional rather than default timers.
- **Mistakes:** no lives. Give immediate hint-rich feedback, a worked step on the second error, a free "fix your mistakes" review, and let mistaken items re-enter the spaced queue.
- **Social:**
  - Class or team weekly goals, e.g., "the class solves 2,000 problems", together with optional small-group (4-6) effort leagues that reset weekly.
  - Rank by effort or improvement, not raw accuracy.
  - Nickname-based display, teacher toggle, student opt-out, no global or stranger leagues.
- **Teacher dashboard:** expose mastery and misconceptions (as ASSISTments does). Avoid shaming views: no public "bottom of class" lists, and keep streaks private by default.
- **Evaluation:** run class-randomized trials measuring delayed learning outcomes, not only DAU or streak length. Watch for novelty decay after 4-8 weeks, and for differential effects on low performers and girls, where evidence is thin.

### Gaps
- No direct experimental evidence was found for the specific parameter values above (streak minimum, freeze count, league size, target success rate for this age). They should be A/B tested.
- No studies were found on gamification design for Israeli students specifically, or on Hebrew/Arabic-language math apps.
